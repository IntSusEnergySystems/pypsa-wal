\begin{abstract}
\noindent
PyPSA-Wal caps Walloon onshore wind at \CapToday{}~MW. That number is an expert
judgement taken from the PNEC wallon and from EDORA; it is not derived from a
land calculation, and the model's own calculation --- the one PyPSA-Eur performs
with \texttt{atlite} --- was never run against Walloon law. This report closes
that gap. It computes the potential from the Walloon Region's own regulatory
cartography: the plan de secteur, the \emph{cadre de référence éolien} of
24~January~2024, the Natura~2000 network, the nature reserves, the landscape
inventories, the natural-hazard maps, the aeronautical obstacle-evaluation map
of the Direction générale Transport Aérien, the LiDAR slope grid and the
classified-site registers, all retrieved live from the Géoportail de la
Wallonie. The constraints are applied in a documented ladder, calibrated against
the \FleetN{} turbines standing in Wallonia today, and turned into capacity by
placing individual machines rather than by multiplying area by a density.

The answer is a range, and the reason it is a range is stated rather than
averaged away. Against the full constraint set, \AdminEligible{}~\si{\square
\kilo\metre} of the Walloon Region survive every exclusion ---
\AdminEligibleShare{}\,\% of the territory. Allocating machines on it at
\PlaceRotorD{}~rotor diameters apart, with no rule about how they group, gives
\FreePnom{}~MW: a land-and-wake bound that puts a near-continuous band of
turbines through every corridor in the Region. Grouping them into farms and
applying the landscape criterion the framework actually states --- at least
\PlaceAzimuth{}° of every village's horizon, within \PlaceHorizonRadius{}~km,
free of turbines --- gives \PlacePnom{}~MW in \PlaceFarms{} farms of
\PlacePerFarm{} machines, against the \FleetFarms{} farms of \FleetPerFarm{}
standing in Wallonia today. A documented allowance for the two constraint
families that still have no public geometry gives a credible technical potential
of \textbf{\CredibleLowGW{}--\CredibleHighGW{}~GW}, central estimate
\CredibleCentralGW{}~GW.

Four results matter more than the headline number.

\textbf{The binding constraint is settlement, then landscape --- not nature and
not the wind.} The setbacks from habitat zones and from scattered dwellings
remove \SetbackCut{}\,\% of the land the plan de secteur admits, in one step.
What then decides the answer is not how much land survives but how the machines
on it may be arranged: the open-horizon criterion alone costs
\HorizonCostPct{}\,\% of the grouped capacity, and the three siting rules
together cost more than the entire constraint ladder below the setbacks.

\textbf{Every constraint in the set is one the standing fleet avoids.} Measured
against the \FleetN{} turbines built in Wallonia, no layer in the reference
constraint set has an avoidance ratio above \FleetMaxRatio{} --- turbines are
systematically less common inside every one of them than the Region average
(§\ref{sec:calibration}).

\textbf{BREGILAB's \BregTotalGW{}~GW is the same quantity as this study's
land-and-wake bound, not as its answer.} This study uses BREGILAB's own
spacing rule, so only the machine separates the two free allocations
($\times\BregStepTurbine$): on their V112 this study's land gives
\BregOursVOneTwelveSpacing{}~MW, so their constraint set is in fact
\emph{tighter} than this one by $\times\BregStepConstraints$. What separates
\BregTotalGW{}~GW from \CredibleCentralGW{}~GW is not the geography: it is that
their allocation carries no landscape rule and this one carries the framework's.

\textbf{PyPSA-Eur's default land analysis is not usable here.} It returns
\BasePnom{}~MW for the Walloon Region, because it admits broad-leaved forest,
ignores the plan de secteur entirely and cannot see scattered housing.
\end{abstract}

\section{The question, and the scope of the answer}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{The number under review}

The Walloon onshore-wind capacity limit in PyPSA-Wal is a single row of
\texttt{config/input\_parameters\_for\_models.csv}:

\begin{quote}\small
\texttt{potential:BEWAL:onwind:p\_nom\_max} $=$ \CapToday{}~MW, source
\emph{``PNEC Wallon et EDORA''}, described as \emph{``potentiel technique,
calculé comme la somme de ce qui est plausible d'atteindre en 2030 comme
capacité et des projets à l'étude''}, with a note that EDORA estimates an
additional 3.8~GW of wind potential by 2050 on the basis of projects under
environmental-impact assessment.
\end{quote}

Two things follow from that wording. It is a \emph{deployment} expectation
assembled from a pipeline of known projects, not a \emph{land} potential; and it
carries no spatial calculation that anyone can re-run or contest. For an
internal scenario exercise that is acceptable. For a number reported to the
administration as the ceiling on what Wallonia can host it is not: the first
question is \emph{``where does \CapTodayGW{}~GW fit, and under which rules?''},
and the current answer is a literature reference.

\subsection{What PyPSA-Eur would have said}

PyPSA-Eur ships exactly the tool needed --- \texttt{atlite}'s land-eligibility
analysis, driven by \texttt{scripts/determine\_availability\_matrix.py}. It is
run for every other node in the model. For the Walloon node its output is
overridden by the CSV row above, so its answer has never been examined. It is
\BasePnom{}~MW (\BasePnomGW{}~GW) for the Walloon Region, on
\BaseEligible{}~\si{\square\kilo\metre} of admitted land.

That the override exists is defensible --- Section~\ref{sec:pypsaeur} shows the
default constraint set is unusable in Wallonia. That the override was never
compared against a corrected calculation is the gap this report fills.

\subsection{What this report answers, and what it does not}

This is a land-eligibility \emph{and siting} study. It answers: how much Walloon
land may legally and physically host a wind turbine, and how many machines can
stand on that land once the framework's own rules about grouping and
inter-distance are applied? It does not answer: how much of that capacity will
be permitted, financed and built, or in what year. A technical potential is an
upper bound on the model's search space, and that is precisely the role
\texttt{p\_nom\_max} plays in PyPSA-Wal --- the build-rate limit and the 2030
corridor, documented in \texttt{docs/renewable-potentials.md}, carry the
deployment question.

Every choice that could have gone the other way is listed in
Section~\ref{sec:hypotheses}, with its direction of effect. There are no open
points in this document: where a constraint could not be measured, a documented
allowance is applied and its size is stated.

\subsection{Which Wallonia}\label{sec:region}

Everything here is computed for the \textbf{administrative Walloon Region}, the
union of the 21 \texttt{BE3} NUTS-3 areas, \adminArea{}~\si{\square\kilo\metre}.
That is the territory the Walloon rules apply to and the territory an
administration will ask about.

It is not the same polygon as the model's \texttt{BEWAL} node. PyPSA-Eur builds
onshore regions by Voronoi-partitioning each country around its substations and
clipping to the country shape; the partition is electrical, not administrative.
The \texttt{BEWAL} polygon covers \modelArea{}~\si{\square\kilo\metre}, or
\modelCoverage{}\,\% of the Region: \areaOutsideModel{}~\si{\square\kilo\metre}
of Walloon territory --- essentially the whole of western Hainaut, from Tournai
and Mouscron to Ath --- sits on the Flemish side of the partition, while
\areaOutsideWallonia{}~\si{\square\kilo\metre} of the \texttt{BEWAL} polygon lies
outside Wallonia, mostly over the French border.

\caveat{A potential computed on one of those two regions and applied to the other
is wrong regardless of how much it moves the number, and the defect affects every
land-based Walloon quantity in PyPSA-Wal --- ground-mounted solar and biomass
among them. Fixing it means correcting the busmap, which is out of scope here and
should be raised separately. Until it is, a figure from this report applied to
\texttt{BEWAL} grants the node the resource of land the model has already given
to Flanders.}

\section{Method}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{The workflow}

The analysis lives in \texttt{workflow\_onwind\_wal/}, a self-contained
Snakemake workflow. It shares nothing with the main PyPSA-Wal workflow except
two read-only inputs: the region shapes and the \texttt{atlite} ERA5 cutout. It
writes only inside its own directory and into
\texttt{docs/onwind\_potential\_wallonia/}. Running it, breaking it or deleting
its outputs cannot affect a model run.

\begin{verbatim}
  cd workflow_onwind_wal
  snakemake -c4 download   # ~20 min, one-off, Walloon open data
  snakemake -c4 all        # eligibility, placement, figures, tables
  snakemake -c4 report     # this PDF
\end{verbatim}

The stages are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Retrieve.} Two dozen reference datasets are pulled from the
    ESRI-REST services of \url{https://geoservices.wallonie.be} (CC-BY~4.0),
    paginated, and written as GeoPackages with a provenance sidecar recording
    the service, the layer, the feature count and the retrieval date
    (Annex~\ref{sec:sources}). Feature counts are checked against the counts
    observed on 22~September~2026 and a deviation above 20\,\% raises a warning:
    if the Region revises a dataset, the workflow says so rather than silently
    changing the answer. Two grids --- the slope-class map and the standing
    fleet --- come from services that are not feature queries and have their own
    retrieval scripts (§\ref{sec:newlayers}, §\ref{sec:calibration}).
  \item \textbf{Exclusion layers.} The regulatory rules are translated into
    geometry, once per turbine class, because most setbacks scale with tip
    height or rotor diameter (Section~\ref{sec:rules}).
  \item \textbf{Eligibility.} \texttt{atlite}'s \texttt{ExclusionContainer}
    burns every layer onto a \SI{100}{\metre} raster in EPSG:3035 and
    \texttt{cutout.availabilitymatrix} aggregates the surviving share per ERA5
    cell --- the identical code path PyPSA-Eur uses, so the two results are
    comparable by construction.
  \item \textbf{Calibration.} The constraint set is tested against the machines
    that are actually standing (§\ref{sec:calibration}).
  \item \textbf{Placement.} Machines, then farms, are allocated on the eligible
    raster (§\ref{sec:placement}). Capacity factors come from the same
    area-and-resource-weighted layout PyPSA-Eur applies.
  \item \textbf{Report.} Tables, figures and every number in this text are
    generated. Nothing here is typed by hand.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Why the constraints are declared in the config, not in the code}

Every exclusion is a block of \texttt{workflow\_onwind\_wal/config.yaml} naming
the dataset, the legal instrument and the buffer. Reviewing the constraint set
--- which is what the administration will want to do --- requires reading one
YAML file, not the Python. Changing a rule is a config edit, and Snakemake
re-derives only what depends on it.

\subsection{From eligible land to capacity: machines are placed, not multiplied}
\label{sec:placement}

The conventional step from eligible area to installable capacity is a
multiplication by a capacity density in MW/\si{\square\kilo\metre}. That
assumes the land arrives in blocks large enough to lay out an array. Walloon
eligible land does not: it arrives as \NPatches{} disconnected patches with a
median size of \MedianPatch{}~ha and a largest of
\LargestPatch{}~\si{\square\kilo\metre}, against
\Footprint{}~\si{\square\kilo\metre} for one cell of a $5D\times7D$ array. An
area-times-density figure therefore counts land no machine could occupy. The
obvious repair --- screening the patches by size --- fails in the other
direction, because a real turbine does not have to sit inside one contiguous
eligible polygon: it may stand at the edge of its patch with its rotor
overhanging ineligible land, and its access track and cabling cross ineligible
land as a matter of course. That is legally correct here, because every Walloon
setback is measured from the \emph{mast}.

This study therefore allocates machines explicitly. Three rules decide how many
fit, and because together they matter more than the whole constraint ladder
below the setbacks, each is justified separately below and each is tested
against the \FleetN{} machines standing in Wallonia (§\ref{sec:calibration}).

\subsubsection{Rule 1 --- how close two machines may stand}

The allocation walks the eligible cells and accepts a machine wherever no
previously accepted machine lies closer than a minimum inter-turbine distance.
That distance is a wake criterion, and three readings of it are carried:

\begin{table}[htbp]\centering\small
\caption{Minimum inter-turbine distance, in rotor diameters, and what it gives
on the reference land before any grouping rule.}
\label{tab:spacing}
\begin{tabular}{llrr}
\toprule
case & basis & $d/D$ & free allocation [\si{\mega\watt}] \\
\midrule
\emph{observed} & median nearest neighbour of the standing Walloon fleet
  & \SpObsD{} & \SpObsFree{} \\
\emph{crosswind} & nearest-neighbour distance of a $5D\times7D$ array; the rule
  BREGILAB uses & \SpCrossD{} & \SpCrossFree{} \\
\emph{isotropic} & $\sqrt{5\times7}\,D$, the area-equivalent of that array
  & \SpIsoD{} & \SpIsoFree{} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\textbf{The reference is \SpCrossD{}\,$D$.} Converting an area-per-turbine into an isotropic \emph{minimum
separation} forbids exactly the pairs the convention it comes from contains: in
a $5D\times7D$ array the closest two machines are $5D$ apart, not
$\sqrt{35}\,D$. It also assumes a two-dimensional array, whereas Walloon wind
farms are predominantly strings along a road, a ridge or a field boundary, where
there is no downwind row and the $7D$ never binds. The physical floor is lower
still --- about $3D$, below which wake-induced turbulence takes a machine
outside the standard IEC~61400-1 load case --- and the standing Walloon fleet
sits at a median of \FleetMachineNNPFifty{}~m between neighbours, about
\SpObsD{}\,$D$ at the \SI{100}{\metre}-class rotor of that fleet, with a
quarter of machines closer than \FleetMachineNNPTwentyFive{}~m.
\SpCrossD{}\,$D$ is therefore already the conservative end of practice.

\subsubsection{Rule 2 --- how many machines make a project}

The free allocation is the land-and-wake bound: \FreePnom{}~MW,
\FreeTurbines{} machines. It is not a plausible build-out, but not for the
reason one might expect. Only \FreeIsolatedPct{}\,\% of its machines are
isolated: the carpet it produces is almost fully connected, a near-continuous
band of turbines across every eligible corridor in the Region. What it lacks is
not company but structure.

Machines are therefore required to come in groups of at least
\PlaceMinTurbines{} within a farm radius. The 2024 framework does define a
\emph{parc éolien} as a project of at least four machines, but that is a
definition of which permitting regime applies, with explicit reductions for
repowering, for extensions of an existing park and for machines above
\SI{3.2}{\mega\watt} --- which the reference class is. The rule is applied here
on empirical grounds instead: \FleetInBigFarms{}\,\% of the standing Walloon
machines are in groups of four or more, so a group of four is what Walloon wind
development actually produces, whatever the regulation permits.

\subsubsection{Rule 3 --- how much of a village's horizon may be occupied}

The rule that decides the answer is the landscape one, and the 2013 cadre de
référence states it precisely:

\begin{quote}\small\itshape
Sauf lorsque les éoliennes sont implantées le long des autoroutes, une référence
indicative à une inter-distance minimale de 4~km à 6~km, en fonction des
résultats de l'étude d'incidence sera prise en considération. Un azimut (ou un
angle horizontal) minimal sans éoliennes doit être préservé pour chaque village;
celui-ci sera d'au moins 130°, sur une distance de 4~km. Un examen de
l'encerclement sera réalisé sur une distance de 9~km dans le cadre de l'EIE.
\end{quote}

Four things in that sentence matter.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item The \SI{4}{}--\SI{6}{\kilo\metre} figure is a \emph{référence
    indicative} that \emph{sera prise en considération} in the light of the
    impact assessment. It is not a minimum distance, and treating it as a hard
    floor between all farms is stricter than the text.
  \item It does not apply \emph{le long des autoroutes} --- and the Walloon
    zoning rule concentrates the eligible land within \SI{1.5}{\kilo\metre} of
    exactly that infrastructure in the first place (§\ref{sec:corridor}), which
    is where \SI{82}{\percent} of the standing fleet stands.
  \item The operative criterion is not a distance between farms at all. It is
    an \textbf{open horizon seen from each village}: at least
    \PlaceAzimuth{}° of azimuth free of turbines, within
    \PlaceHorizonRadius{}~km.
  \item All of it is from the 2013 framework. The framework adopted on
    24~January~2024, in force since 25~April~2024, replaced it and does not
    carry the inter-distance; it asks instead for grouping along the landscape's
    structuring lines and for village encirclement to be assessed.
\end{enumerate}

The open-horizon criterion is therefore implemented as written. Every village
--- a contiguous block of plan-de-secteur habitat zoning above
\SI{10}{\hectare}, \HorizonSettlements{} of them --- carries the arcs its
surrounding machines occupy, each machine subtending the angle its rotor covers
from the village centre. A candidate farm is refused if admitting it would take
any village's largest turbine-free arc below \PlaceAzimuth{}°. Farms are still
required not to overlap, at \PlaceInterfarm{}~km between centres, which is the
geometry of the standing fleet and not a rule (§\ref{sec:calibration}).

The \SI{4}{}--\SI{6}{\kilo\metre} inter-distance is reported only as a
policy sensitivity: what the answer would be if that indicative reference were
applied as a binding minimum.

\finding{Of the three rules, the third is the expensive one. Machines grouped
into farms but with no landscape criterion give \NoHorizonPnom{}~MW; the
open-horizon test refuses \HorizonRefused{} candidate farms and brings that to
\PlacePnom{}~MW, \HorizonCostPct{}\,\% less. Applying the indicative
\SI{4}{}--\SI{6}{\kilo\metre} inter-distance on top would take it to
\IfdSixMW{}--\IfdFourMW{}~MW. An onshore-wind potential quoted without saying
which landscape rule it carries cannot be compared with anything.}

\section{The Walloon regulatory framework}\label{sec:rules}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{Sources of law and of practice}

Five instruments define where a wind turbine may stand in Wallonia:

\begin{description}
  \item[CoDT] the \emph{Code du Développement Territorial}, which fixes the
    zoning categories of the \emph{plan de secteur} and, at art.~R.II.21-1, the
    definition of the \emph{principales infrastructures de communication} (PIC):
    motorways and regional link roads, railways, and navigable waterways. Its
    art.~D.II.37~§1 al.~6 also requires the mast to stand outside a recognised
    conservation site, within \SI{750}{\metre} of a PIC, and outside a
    broad-leaved stand in the sense of the Code forestier.
  \item[Cadre de référence éolien] the reference framework for wind
    development. The 2013 version imposed a setback from plan-de-secteur habitat
    zones of four times the \emph{mast} height --- about \SI{600}{\metre} for the
    machines of the time --- and the 2022 update of the favourable-zone map
    applied it as four times the \emph{total} height. It was replaced by the
    framework adopted on 24~January~2024, in force since 25~April~2024, which
    sets that distance at \textbf{\SI{500}{\metre} plus half the total height},
    applies it to \emph{zone d'habitat}, \emph{zone d'habitat à caractère
    rural}, \emph{zone d'aménagement communal concerté} destined for housing and
    \emph{zone d'habitat vert}, and lowers the definition of a wind farm from
    five turbines to four. The same text carries the regroupement principle and
    the indicative \SI{4}{}--\SI{6}{\kilo\metre} inter-distance between farms.
  \item[Aeronautical servitudes] the obstacle-evaluation map of the federal
    Direction générale Transport Aérien, published by the Region as
    \emph{Hauteurs pouvant impacter l'aviation}.
  \item[Sectoral conditions and permitting practice] noise limits, defence
    radar clearances, and the protection of the Humain radio-astronomy station
    and the Wideumont weather radar.
  \item[The Walloon favourable-zone cartography] the 2013 map of \emph{zones
    favorables} and its 2022 update by SPW and Gembloux Agro-Bio~Tech, which is
    the Region's own operational translation of all of the above into GIS
    layers. Its constraint tables are the template followed here.
\end{description}

\subsection{The one zoning rule that dominates: the agricultural corridor}
\label{sec:corridor}

The Walloon methodology does not treat agricultural land as uniformly available.
It admits it only within \SI{1.5}{\kilo\metre} of a PIC or of an
economic-activity zone, and coniferous forest only within \SI{750}{\metre} of a
PIC. This is a \emph{concentration} rule --- keep wind development along the
existing structuring infrastructure rather than scattered across the open
landscape --- and it has no analogue anywhere in PyPSA-Eur. It also works in the
opposite direction to PyPSA-Eur's own treatment, which \emph{excludes} a
\SI{1}{\kilo\metre} band around transport infrastructure.

\subsection{Setbacks for the three turbine classes}

The turbine classes mirror the three \emph{scénarios de base} of the 2022
favourable-zone update (total tip height 150, 180 and 210~m) so the two studies
can be read side by side. The reference class is \refTurbine{}: rotor
\RotorDiameter{}~m, tip height \TipHeight{}~m, giving a habitat-zone setback of
\HabitatSetback{}~m under the 2024 framework.

\caveat{Read as $4H$ on the total height --- the form the 2022 favourable-zone
update used --- the 2024 framework is the \emph{looser} of the two for every
machine considered here: \HabitatSetback{}~m against \SI{740}{\metre} for the
reference class. Results computed on the 2013 convention would be lower. The
2024 rule is used because it is the one in force; the 2013 rule is available in
the config (\texttt{setbacks.habitat\_zone.default: cdr2013}).}

\subsection{The three constraint families added from data that had to be
rebuilt}\label{sec:newlayers}

Three of the families in Table~\ref{tab:rules} are not published as ready-made
exclusion polygons. They are reconstructed here, and each reconstruction is a
hypothesis in its own right.

\paragraph{Aeronautical servitudes.} The Region publishes
\emph{Hauteurs pouvant impacter l'aviation} (AOEM), which gives, for every point
of Wallonia, the height above which the Direction générale Transport Aérien must
be consulted. The six classes --- 0, 15, 25, 35, 50 and 60~m --- tile the
territory exactly. Four of them are nested rings around the aerodromes (Liège,
Charleroi, Beauvechain, Florennes, Chièvres, Saint-Hubert, Bertrix, Spa and the
smaller fields); two are the territory-wide notification background, one polygon
of \SI{8045}{\square\kilo\metre} at 25~m and one of
\SI{1556}{\square\kilo\metre} at 60~m. Any wind turbine exceeds every one of
those thresholds, so the map is a consultation duty and not, on its face, an
exclusion. Which of the aerodrome rings is a genuine no-go is settled
empirically in §\ref{sec:calibration}: the 0~m and 15~m rings are strongly
avoided by the standing fleet and are excluded here; the 35~m and 50~m rings
carry \emph{more} turbines per square kilometre than Wallonia as a whole and are
not.

\paragraph{The \SI{7}{\percent} slope criterion.} The 2013 Walloon methodology
treats slopes above \SI{7}{\percent} as incompatible with the crane pad and
access track of a large machine. The Region publishes the slope grid ---
\texttt{ERRUISOL\_CLASSE\_PENTE}, per cent slope derived from the
\SI{10}{\metre} ERRUISSOL terrain model --- but only as a visualisation service,
with no feature query and no coverage download. What the service does expose is
an image export that renders an exact bounding box into an exact number of
pixels with nearest-neighbour resampling and a seven-colour palette, one colour
per slope class. Decoding those colours recovers the class grid losslessly, and
the \SI{7}{\percent} threshold falls exactly on a class boundary, so no
interpolation is involved. The grid is rendered at \SI{25}{\metre} and a
\SI{100}{\metre} cell is excluded when the \emph{majority} of its samples are at
or above \SI{7}{\percent} --- that is, when most of the hectare a turbine needs
is too steep, not when a single steep pixel touches it. On that rule
\SI{37.5}{\percent} of the Walloon Region is excluded, and the reconstruction
passes two independent checks: \SI{99}{\percent} of the cells the Region's
separate $>30^\circ$ vector layer flags are inside it, and its north/south split
(\SI{19.5}{\percent} of the northern third against \SI{49.3}{\percent} of the
southern third) is the Hesbaye--Ardenne gradient.

\paragraph{Radar and radio astronomy.} skeyes and Defence do not publish
radar-constraint geometry, and the AOEM map covers obstacle-limitation surfaces,
not radar interference. Three installations whose protection perimeters fall
inside Wallonia and are not already covered by an aerodrome ring are
reconstructed as circles at their published coordinates: the Wideumont C-band
weather radar (\SI{5}{\kilo\metre}, WMO/OPERA guidance), the Humain
radio-astronomy station (\SI{3}{\kilo\metre}) and the Bertem primary/secondary
surveillance radar (\SI{10}{\kilo\metre}, of which only the southern arc reaches
Walloon Brabant). Together they remove \ServRadarRemoved{}~\si{\square
\kilo\metre}, so the approximation is not load-bearing.

\subsection{The forest species split}\label{sec:forest}

The CoDT excludes a mast standing in a broad-leaved stand. The plan de secteur
records forest zoning but not species, so the split has to come from a land-cover
product. This study uses CORINE grid codes 23/24/25 at \SI{250}{\metre}, 2006
vintage, counting mixed stands as broad-leaved --- the conservative choice, and
the same source PyPSA-Eur uses.

Two better sources were identified and neither can be used:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{CARTOFOR}, the continuous mapping of the Walloon forest by
    Gembloux Agro-Bio~Tech under the Accord-Cadre de recherche forestière, has a
    2016 three-class product distinguishing broad-leaved from coniferous stands.
    Its own documentation states that the cartographic products are undergoing
    validation and will be made available on the Region's portal in due course;
    they are not published today, and no service exposes them. It is therefore
    recorded here as a named future refinement, not as an open point.
  \item \textbf{WALOUS 2018}, the Walloon land-cover grid, does carry explicit
    \emph{Feuillus} and \emph{Résineux} classes at a far finer resolution than
    CORINE, and would be the correct implementation of the CoDT rule ---
    including for the broad-leaved woodlots that sit inside agricultural zones,
    which the present implementation misses. Its service answers point queries
    but returns a blank image to the bulk export that the slope grid accepts, so
    it cannot be retrieved as a grid. Obtaining it is an administrative step.
\end{itemize}

\caveat{The stake is bounded. The species split only decides the status of
plan-de-secteur forest zones lying within \SI{750}{\metre} of a PIC, which is a
small share of a Region whose eligible land is overwhelmingly agricultural; and
the direction of the error from using CORINE is not knowable without the better
data, because a coarse raster both admits broad-leaved cells and rejects
coniferous ones.}

% =====================================================================
\section{PyPSA-Eur's default constraint set is not applicable in Wallonia}
\label{sec:pypsaeur}
% =====================================================================

PyPSA-Eur's onshore-wind land analysis (\texttt{config/config.default.yaml},
section \texttt{renewable.onwind}) admits CORINE grid codes 12--29, 31 and 32,
excludes a \SI{1}{\kilo\metre} buffer around codes 1--6, excludes Natura~2000,
and assumes \SI{3}{\mega\watt\per\square\kilo\metre}. Five features of that
specification make it unsuitable here.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{It admits all forest.} Codes 23, 24 and 25 are broad-leaved,
    coniferous and mixed forest. Wallonia is roughly one third forest, and the
    forest is concentrated in the Ardennes --- the windiest part of the Region.
    Walloon law excludes broad-leaved stands outright and admits coniferous
    stands only near a PIC. This single difference moves the answer more than
    any other.
  \item \textbf{It cannot see scattered housing.} CORINE's minimum mapping unit
    is \SI{25}{\hectare}. Walloon \emph{habitat dispersé} --- isolated farms and
    ribbon development --- is invisible to it, and it is exactly what the
    \SI{400}{\metre} rule protects. The \SI{1}{\kilo\metre} buffer around CORINE
    urban codes is not a substitute: it is generous where settlements are large
    and blind where they are not.
  \item \textbf{It has no zoning.} The plan de secteur is the legal instrument
    that decides admissibility in Wallonia, and CORINE is a land-\emph{cover}
    product that knows nothing about it.
  \item \textbf{Its buffer around transport infrastructure has the wrong sign.}
    PyPSA-Eur excludes \SI{1}{\kilo\metre} around roads and railways; the
    Walloon rule requires agricultural sites to be \emph{within}
    \SI{1.5}{\kilo\metre} of them.
  \item \textbf{The CORINE raster shipped in the data bundle is
    \SI{250}{\metre}, 2006 vintage} (\texttt{g250\_clc06\_V18\_5.tif}). At
    \SI{250}{\metre} a single pixel is \SI{6.25}{\hectare}; at the scale of a
    \SI{400}{\metre} setback that is a coarse instrument, and the land cover is
    twenty years old.
\end{enumerate}

\finding{PyPSA-Eur's default land analysis admits
\BaseEligible{}~\si{\square\kilo\metre} of Wallonia and returns
\BasePnom{}~MW. That figure should not be quoted as a Walloon technical
potential, and the decision to override it in
\texttt{input\_parameters\_for\_models.csv} was correct. What was missing was a
corrected calculation to override it \emph{with}.}

\section{Calibration against the standing fleet}\label{sec:calibration}
% =====================================================================

A land-eligibility study is a claim about where a machine may be built. The
cheapest way to find out whether the claim is calibrated is to point it at the
machines that exist. The Region does not publish turbine coordinates;
OpenStreetMap does, and its coverage of the Belgian fleet is essentially
complete. \FleetN{} turbines are mapped inside the Walloon Region. They are used
only to test the constraint set --- never as an input to the potential.

For each constraint layer, Table~\ref{tab:fleet} reports the share of the Region
it covers, the share of the standing fleet it covers, and the ratio of the two.
A ratio well below one says that developers and the permitting authority treat
the constraint as real. A ratio at or above one says that they do not, whatever
the layer is called.

several layers that permitting practice treats as \emph{partial} constraints
must be binary in an area calculation. It does mean the potential computed here
is conservative relative to what has actually been permitted, and it bounds by
how much: a constraint set calibrated to reproduce historical permitting would
admit roughly twice as much land.}

\subsection{The residual allowance}\label{sec:residual}

Two families of the Walloon method have no public geometry and both reduce the
potential. They are represented by a multiplicative allowance on the land that
survives, with a central value and a bracket:

\begin{description}
  \item[Priority ornithological zones, \ResOrnithology{}\,\% (bracket
    0--20\,\%).] The DEMNA high-priority bird layer is an integral exclusion of
    the favourable-zone method and is not published. It was built largely from
    concentrations of sensitive species, which in Wallonia coincide to a large
    extent with the Natura~2000 bird SPAs and the wetlands already excluded
    here; the incremental area is the non-Natura remainder. The central value is
    a judgement, not a measurement, and it is stated as one.
  \item[Partial constraints, \ResPartial{}\,\% (bracket 0--30\,\%).] The method
    distinguishes integral from \emph{partial} exclusions --- medium-priority
    bird zones, bat-interest zones, migration corridors, the principal
    ecological structure, radar study zones between 8 and \SI{16}{\kilo\metre}
    --- and grants turbines falling in them a 25\,\% success rate. Their
    combined footprint is unknown; the central estimate assumes they cover a
    fifth of the surviving land, so the expected loss is
    $0.20\times0.75 = 15\,\%$.
\end{description}

Together they leave \ResSurvival{}\,\% of the farm-model capacity in the central
case. One further criterion --- the azimuth of open horizon the framework asks
to be preserved for each village --- is a site-level test that neither an area
method nor a farm-level allocation can represent; it is not given an allowance,
and it pushes the same way as the other two.

\subsection{Full-load hours and energy}

The capacity factor is computed on the 2013 ERA5/SARAH-3 cutout with the same
layout weighting PyPSA-Eur uses. For the reference case it is \AdminFlh{}~h,
which with the farm-model capacity gives \PlaceEnergy{}~TWh/yr gross and
\ResCentralEnergy{}~TWh/yr after the residual allowance --- against the
\TargetTWh{}~TWh/yr of the PNEC/PACE 2030 target.

Those full-load hours are high, and the reason is the machine, not the method.
The reference class is an NREL~2020~ATB \SI{4}{\mega\watt} turbine with a
\SI{150}{\metre} rotor --- a specific power of
\SI{226}{\watt\per\square\metre}, against \SI{305}{\watt\per\square\metre} for
the V112 PyPSA-Eur uses by default --- on a \SI{110}{\metre} hub instead of
\SI{80}{\metre}. Low-specific-power machines on tall towers are exactly what
raises full-load hours, and it is why the same workflow returns
\VOneTwelveFlh{}~h when the V112 is substituted into the same eligible land. The
V112 number is the one that reconciles with §\ref{sec:validation} and with the
model's own resource.

\caveat{These are \emph{gross} new-turbine full-load hours: no wake, availability
or electrical losses are applied, which is PyPSA-Eur's convention for
\texttt{p\_max\_pu} and therefore the convention PyPSA-Wal needs. They are not
comparable with the 1\,885~h TIMES-WAL uses for the blended existing fleet, nor
with what the standing Walloon fleet delivers; that discrepancy is analysed in
\texttt{docs/renewable-potentials.md}~§10. BREGILAB applies a calibrated
performance ratio of \BregPerf{} to the same kind of figure
(§\ref{sec:bregilab}), which is the right correction for a study reporting
delivered energy.}

\caveat{Only the 2013 weather year is available on this machine. The Walloon
onshore-wind resource in 2013 is about 5\,\% \emph{better} than in 2010, the
year the main model runs use (\texttt{docs/renewable-potentials.md}~§10.1).
Capacity is unaffected --- it depends only on the land --- but the energy column
is optimistic by roughly that margin relative to a 2010 run.}

\subsection{BREGILAB / VITO Dynamic Energy Atlas}\label{sec:bregilab}

The other published bottom-up estimate of the Walloon onshore-wind potential is
the BREGILAB project's, computed with VITO's Dynamic Energy Atlas
(Clymans et al., 2022, WP3, Table~22). Its Table~22 gives Wallonia
\BregCurrent{}~MW current and \BregAdditional{}~MW additional onshore wind; the
\BregTotalGW{}~GW quoted in its running text is their sum, that is, a
\emph{gross} potential on the same convention as \texttt{p\_nom\_max}. It is
$\times\BregRatio{}$ this study's free allocation, $\times\BregVsFarm{}$ its
farm allocation and $\times\BregVsCentral{}$ its central estimate, and the whole
of the difference can be accounted for.

\paragraph{Method.} BREGILAB delineates available space from the 2013 cadre de
référence and the 2009 ICEDD study, on a \SI{100}{\metre} grid, then runs an
allocation algorithm that packs in as many Vestas V112 (3.3~MW, hub
\SI{94}{\metre}) as will fit subject to a minimum inter-turbine distance of
$5D = \SI{560}{\metre}$. Constraints are weighted rather than binary:
air-traffic and military \emph{orange} zones and the Walloon protected views
around urban settlements carry a weight of 0.4 and bind only in combination with
something else. Production is corrected by a performance ratio of \BregPerf{},
calibrated against Elia's measured output --- which is why its reported
availability factor for the Walloon potential is \BregFlh{}~h and not the
\BregFlhGross{}~h implied gross, a figure close to what this workflow gets for
the same machine, and a useful independent check that the two resource models
agree.

allocation onto BREGILAB's conventions. Both factors are exact.

\begin{description}
  \item[Turbine class, $\times\BregStepTurbine$.] The V112 is a
    \SI{136}{\metre} machine. Under the 2024 framework its habitat setback is
    \HabitatSetbackVOneTwelve{}~m against \HabitatSetback{}~m for the reference
    class, so more land survives: \BregOursArea{}~\si{\square\kilo\metre}
    against \AdminEligible{}, and at a fixed $5D$ the smaller rotor also packs
    tighter. The two effects compound, which is why the turbine class is not a
    detail when comparing two potentials. It is the only convention
    difference: both studies use a $5D$ spacing rule.
  \item[Constraint set, $\times\BregStepConstraints$.] What is left. It is
    \emph{below} one: with BREGILAB's machine and BREGILAB's spacing, this
    study's eligible land carries \BregOursVOneTwelveSpacing{}~MW against their
    \BregTotal{}~MW. Their constraint set is therefore the tighter of the two,
    which is the opposite of what the headline numbers suggest.
\end{description}

\paragraph{Why their constraint set is tighter, and ours is not uniformly
looser.} The components run in both directions and roughly cancel, with theirs
ahead. BREGILAB applies civil-aviation and military \emph{red} zones and
SEVESO, pipeline and nuclear setbacks that this study does not have at all; it
applies the 2013 habitat rule, which for its V112 on a \SI{94}{\metre} hub is
\SI{600}{\metre}, against the \HabitatSetbackVOneTwelve{}~m the 2024 rule gives
for the \SI{80}{\metre}-hub V112 used here; and it applies a
\SI{2}{\kilo\metre} exclusion around nuclear facilities. Against that, it
treats the Walloon landscape perimeters and protected views at weight 0.4
rather than as integral exclusions, applies no flood layer in Wallonia, and
does not apply the \SI{7}{\percent} slope criterion --- which alone is worth
\ServSlopePct{}\,\% of the land here.

\finding{The two studies do not disagree about Walloon geography. They disagree
about what a technical potential is. BREGILAB reports the maximum number of
machines that can be packed into the available space under a wake-avoidance
distance; this study reports the number that can stand there once the
framework's own grouping and landscape rules are applied. On BREGILAB's
convention this study's land gives \BregOursVOneTwelveSpacing{}~MW, more than
their \BregTotal{}~MW; on this study's convention the same land gives
\PlacePnom{}~MW. The factor between those two numbers --- roughly three --- is
the whole of the disagreement, and it is a modelling convention, not a fact
about Wallonia.}

\paragraph{What was adopted from it.} Three things, and together they changed
the answer of this study more than any new data layer did:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Allocating machines instead of multiplying area by a density.}
    This is BREGILAB's method and it is better than an area calculation on
    fragmented land, and it removes the need for any patch-size screen.
  \item \textbf{Graded rather than binary constraints.} BREGILAB's weighting of
    consultation zones is the idea behind this study's separate treatment of the
    aeronautical rings and behind the residual allowance for partial
    constraints.
  \item \textbf{Validating against measured reality.} BREGILAB calibrates its
    production against Elia. This study cannot do that for a potential, but the
    same instinct produced the fleet calibration of §\ref{sec:calibration},
    which removed two constraints that had no empirical support and confirmed
    the rest.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{What was not adopted, and why.} BREGILAB's performance ratio of
\BregPerf{} is a calibration factor that absorbs both physical losses and the
bias of its own wind model against Elia's measurements. Applying it to a
PyPSA-Eur profile would double-count, because PyPSA-Wal expects a gross
\texttt{p\_max\_pu}. Its weighted-overlay treatment of soft constraints was also
not reproduced literally: with only two soft families left unmapped here, an
explicit allowance with a stated bracket (§\ref{sec:residual}) is more
transparent than a hidden weight.

\subsection{The 2022 SPW/Gembloux favourable-zone update}

That study reports \SpwStrictArea{}~\si{\square\kilo\metre} of \emph{zones
favorables strictes} and \SpwEnlargedArea{}~\si{\square\kilo\metre} of
\emph{zones favorables élargies} for its ``180~m'' case, and \SpwNewGWh{}~GWh/yr
of \emph{additional} production from 457 potential turbines, cut down from 1\,323
simulated ones by landscape inter-distance and encirclement criteria.

Three differences are structural:

\begin{itemize}
  \item It applies the priority bird zones and the azimuth-of-open-horizon test
    that this study represents only by an allowance and by the inter-farm
    distance --- the criteria responsible for its own cut from 1\,323 to 457.
  \item It counts only \emph{new} capacity, excluding the footprint of the
    standing fleet, whereas this study reports a gross potential.
  \item It applies a wind-resource threshold; this study applies none.
\end{itemize}

Adding the standing fleet back to its 457 turbines gives of order
\SI{4}{\giga\watt} on a comparable gross basis, against this study's
\CredibleCentralGW{}~GW central estimate, for the structural reasons listed
above.

\subsection{The model's own cap}

\CapToday{}~MW against this study's central \ResCentral{}~MW is a factor of
\CapRatioCentral{}, and \CapToday{}~MW lies inside the credible range
\CredibleLow{}--\CredibleHigh{}~MW. Two qualifications matter.

First, the cap was constructed as a deployment expectation --- what is plausible
by 2030 plus the project pipeline --- and this report produces a land-and-siting
envelope. That the two land in the same place is a coincidence of construction,
not a confirmation.

Second, \CapToday{}~MW is above the gross reference case
(\PlacePnom{}~MW) and is reachable only by dropping the open-horizon criterion
altogether, which would give \NoHorizonPnom{}~MW, and then allowing for none of
the unmapped constraint families. If instead the 2013 indicative inter-distance
were applied as a rule, the envelope would be \LegacyLow{}--\LegacyHigh{}~MW
and the cap would be roughly twice it.

\subsection{Three independent cross-checks}\label{sec:crosschecks}

The conclusion of §\ref{sec:answer} rests on a chain --- constraint set,
eligible raster, machine allocation, farm allocation, residual allowance ---
and an error anywhere in it would move the answer. Three checks test the end of
the chain against things that are known independently of it.

\paragraph{The size of a Walloon wind farm.} The farm model predicts
\PlacePerFarm{} machines per farm; the \FleetN{} standing turbines group into
farms of \FleetPerFarm{} (§\ref{sec:fleetgeometry}). The model reproduces the
layout the Walloon landscape and the Walloon rules actually produce, which is
the single strongest evidence that the allocation is not an artefact. Note that
this is not circular: the farm \emph{radius} is calibrated on the fleet, but
the number of machines that fit inside it is not --- that comes from the
eligible raster and the wake spacing.

\paragraph{The number of wind farms.} The model places \PlaceFarms{} farms
against the \FleetFarms{} that exist, i.e. it says Wallonia could host of order
twice as many wind farms as it has. That is the
right order for a technical potential: much more than a factor two would be
implausible in a Region this settled, and less than one would contradict the
existence of a project pipeline.

\paragraph{The resulting deployment density, against a country that has built
one.} Germany ended 2024 with \SI{63.55}{\giga\watt} of onshore wind in
\num{28717} machines on \SI{357600}{\square\kilo\metre} --- a deployed density of
\DeDensity{}~kW/\si{\square\kilo\metre} and \DeTurbines{} machines per
\SI{1000}{\square\kilo\metre}. Wallonia today carries
\WalDensityToday{}~kW/\si{\square\kilo\metre}, \TodayVsDe{} times that. This
study's central estimate corresponds to
\WalDensityCentral{}~kW/\si{\square\kilo\metre}, \CentralVsDe{} times Germany's
current deployed density, and the top of its range to \HighVsDe{} times.

\finding{A technical potential that lands at \CentralVsDe{} times the density
Germany has \emph{already built} is in the right place: comfortably above what
Wallonia has done, not far above what a comparable country has done, and well
below the densities reached in Schleswig-Holstein or Lower Saxony. Had the
method returned a figure at ten times Germany's deployed density, it would have
been describing an arithmetic exercise rather than a landscape.}

\caveat{The farm allocation is greedy, not optimal: it is a heuristic that
places farms in decreasing order of surrounding eligible land and never revisits
a choice. An exact packing would fit somewhat more, so the farm figure is a
slight under-estimate of what the rules permit --- in the opposite direction to
every other approximation in this report.}

\section{Hypotheses}\label{sec:hypotheses}
% =====================================================================

Every choice below is a config entry, every one can be varied, and each is
stated with the direction in which it moves the result.

\begin{description}
  \item[Region.] The administrative Walloon Region, not the model's
    \texttt{BEWAL} node (§\ref{sec:region}). \emph{A \texttt{BEWAL} figure would
    be about \SI{10}{\percent} lower.}
  \item[Capacity comes from placing machines,] not from multiplying area by a
    density (§\ref{sec:placement}). \emph{The density convention would give
    \AdminPnom{}~MW, about half the reference case, for reasons that have
    nothing to do with the land.}
  \item[Minimum inter-turbine distance] \PlaceRotorD{}\,$D$ = \PlaceSpacing{}~m,
    the nearest-neighbour distance of a $5D\times7D$ array and the rule VITO
    uses. \emph{The physical floor is about $3D$; the standing Walloon fleet
    sits at \SpObsD{}\,$D$; the area-equivalent $\sqrt{5\times7}\,D$ would give
    \SpIsoMW{}~MW instead of \SpCrossMW{}~MW and is an over-reading
    (§\ref{sec:placement}).}
  \item[Minimum farm size] \PlaceMinTurbines{} machines, applied because
    \FleetInBigFarms{}\,\% of the standing fleet is in groups that size, not
    because the 2024 definition of a \emph{parc éolien} forbids fewer --- it
    does not, and it exempts machines above \SI{3.2}{\mega\watt} in any case.
  \item[Farm radius and separation] \PlaceRadius{}~m and \PlaceInterfarm{}~km,
    the observed p75 farm radius and twice it, which sits inside the observed
    p10--p25 band of farm separations (§\ref{sec:fleetgeometry}).
    \emph{Calibrated, not imposed: the model forbids no farm geometry Wallonia
    has permitted.}
  \item[Landscape criterion] the open horizon of the 2013 framework ---
    \PlaceAzimuth{}° free within \PlaceHorizonRadius{}~km of every village above
    \SI{10}{\hectare}. \emph{The single most expensive siting rule,
    \HorizonCostPct{}\,\% of the grouped capacity, and the one the standing
    fleet demonstrably respects (§\ref{sec:horizoncheck}).}
  \item[The 2013 indicative \SI{4}{}--\SI{6}{\kilo\metre} inter-distance is
    \emph{not} applied.] It is an indicative reference subordinate to the impact
    assessment, it does not apply along motorways, and the 2024 framework that
    replaced the 2013 one does not carry it. \emph{Applying it as a rule would
    give \LegacyLow{}--\LegacyHigh{}~MW instead of \ResCentral{}~MW.}
  \item[Habitat setback] the 2024 framework, $500 + H/2$. \emph{The 2013 rule
    ($4H$) is more restrictive for every class considered here.}
  \item[Scattered dwellings] \SI{400}{\metre} around every ICAR address point.
    The address layer includes non-residential buildings, so this is mildly
    conservative; the 2022 SPW study makes the same approximation.
  \item[Aeronautical servitudes] the AOEM rings at 0 and \SI{15}{\metre} only,
    after the fleet calibration of §\ref{sec:calibration}. \emph{Including the
    35 and \SI{50}{\metre} rings would remove a further
    \SI{89}{\square\kilo\metre}, against the evidence.}
  \item[Slope] $\geq 7\,\%$ on the ERRUISSOL \SI{10}{\metre} grid, majority rule
    over the \SI{25}{\metre} samples in each \SI{100}{\metre} cell. \emph{The
    threshold is a 2013 criterion and is resolution-dependent; a coarser terrain
    model would exclude less.}
  \item[Radar and radio astronomy] three circles at published coordinates with
    generic separation distances (§\ref{sec:newlayers}). \emph{Worth
    \ServRadarRemoved{}~\si{\square\kilo\metre}, so not load-bearing.}
  \item[Broad-leaved / coniferous split] CORINE at \SI{250}{\metre}, mixed
    stands counted as broad-leaved; CARTOFOR and WALOUS would both be better and
    neither is retrievable (§\ref{sec:forest}). \emph{Direction unknown.}
  \item[Residual allowance] \ResOrnithology{}\,\% for priority bird zones and
    \ResPartial{}\,\% for partial constraints, both bracketed
    (§\ref{sec:residual}). \emph{Both reduce; the brackets are carried into the
    headline range.}
  \item[Azimuth of open horizon] not represented. \emph{Reduces; it is what cut
    the Region's own 2022 study from 1\,323 turbines to 457.}
  \item[Runoff flood hazard] excluded from the flood layer; the overflow extent
    at the extreme return period is used instead.
  \item[Geometry generalisation] heavy polygon layers are requested with
    \SI{25}{\metre} server-side generalisation --- a quarter of the exclusion
    raster cell, so it cannot change the aggregate.
  \item[Existing wind farms are not excluded.] The result is a \emph{gross}
    potential: the standing \Installed{}~MW occupies land that is counted as
    available. This is the right convention for \texttt{p\_nom\_max}, which caps
    the total fleet, not additions.
  \item[Repowering is not a separate term.] A land-and-siting study measures the
    envelope, not the turnover of the fleet inside it. EDORA's additional
    3.8~GW by 2050 explicitly includes repowering, which is part of why that
    number and this one are not the same kind of quantity.
  \item[No grid-connection or economic screen.] Distance to the HV network,
    connection cost and wind-speed profitability thresholds are not applied.
    \emph{All reduce.}
  \item[Energy is gross.] No wake, availability or electrical losses, matching
    PyPSA-Eur's \texttt{p\_max\_pu} convention. \emph{A delivered-energy figure
    would be roughly \SI{15}{\percent} lower; BREGILAB's calibrated performance
    ratio is \BregPerf{}.}
  \item[Weather year 2013.] Capacity is unaffected; energy is about 5\,\%
    optimistic relative to the 2010 year the model runs use.
\end{description}

\finding{Almost every hypothesis in this list pushes the same way: down. The
figure in §\ref{sec:answer} is an upper bound on a technical potential, and the
constraint families that could not be mapped, the criteria that need a
site-level model, and the economic screens that were not applied would all
reduce it further.}

\section{What this means for \texttt{p\_nom\_max}}
% =====================================================================

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The cap can be documented rather than cited.}
    \CapToday{}~MW is reachable within this framework: it corresponds to the
    \SI{4}{\kilo\metre} inter-farm distance with a small residual allowance, on
    \AdminEligible{}~\si{\square\kilo\metre} of eligible land carrying
    \IfdFourFarms{} farms of \refTurbine{}. That is a statement the administration can contest on
    its merits. Replacing the CSV's ``PNEC wallon et EDORA'' with that derivation
    costs nothing and removes the main weakness of the current number.
  \item \textbf{If a single value is wanted, \ResCentral{}~MW is the defensible
    one} --- the farm model at the framework's permissive inter-distance with the
    central residual allowance. It is below the current cap by
    \CapRatioCentral{}$\times$, and the difference is entirely the two unmapped
    constraint families.
  \item \textbf{Settle the region question before quoting anything externally.}
    A Region figure applied to the \texttt{BEWAL} node overstates the node by
    about \SI{10}{\percent} (§\ref{sec:region}).
  \item \textbf{Decide what \texttt{p\_nom\_max} is meant to be.} PyPSA-Wal
    already carries a build-rate limit and a 2030 corridor
    (\texttt{docs/renewable-potentials.md}~§3--4) to represent deployment. If
    those are doing their job, \texttt{p\_nom\_max} should be the technical
    potential and the present value is the wrong kind of number in the right
    range. That is a modelling decision, not a GIS one.
\end{enumerate}

% =====================================================================
\section{What would sharpen the number further}
% =====================================================================

None of these is a gap that invalidates the result; each would narrow the range.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Ask SPW Énergie for the DEMNA priority-bird layer and the
    partial-constraint layers.} They exist and the favourable-zone cartography
    uses them. They would replace the residual allowance, which is the widest
    single term in the range, with geometry.
  \item \textbf{Ask SPW Territoire how the 2024 framework's encirclement test is
    applied in practice.} The 2013 text gave a \PlaceAzimuth{}° open horizon
    over \PlaceHorizonRadius{}~km and an encirclement examination over
    \SI{9}{\kilo\metre}; the 2024 text asks for encirclement to be assessed
    without fixing either number. The open-horizon threshold is worth
    \HorizonCostPct{}\,\% of the answer, and the \SI{9}{\kilo\metre}
    examination is not represented at all.
  \item \textbf{Request WALOUS 2018 as a grid, or CARTOFOR when it is
    published.} Either would replace a 2006 \SI{250}{\metre} raster with a
    Walloon product and would implement the CoDT broad-leaved rule where it
    currently is not applied at all (§\ref{sec:forest}).
  \item \textbf{Run the azimuth-of-open-horizon test on the placed farms.} The
    farm layout is written out as
    \texttt{results/placement\_\{turbine\}.gpkg} precisely so that a
    landscape pass can be run on it. This is the one remaining criterion of the
    Walloon method that is not represented at all.
  \item \textbf{Repeat on the 2010 weather year} once its cutout is available,
    to put the energy column on the same footing as the model runs.
\end{enumerate}
